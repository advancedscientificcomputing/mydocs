At the center of my work is the direct link between theoretical inquiry and real-world application. This requires continuous movement through a clear cycle: learn, unlearn, and relearn. Learning expands capacity by acquiring new knowledge, skills, and perspectives. Unlearning is the deliberate release of beliefs, habits, and methods that no longer deliver results. It demands honesty to recognize outdated patterns and the willingness to suspend them without discarding useful wisdom. Relearning installs improved approaches that combine retained experience with updated understanding. These three processes form a continuous cycle rather than isolated steps. Learning alone accumulates without refinement. Unlearning alone empties. Relearning without prior release collides with residual patterns. The cycle supports permanent adaptation under changing conditions.

I apply the same discipline through the Reverse Engineering Lifecycle. Reverse engineering is the systematic process of dissecting an existing product, system, or method to understand its design, structure, and function. It moves from finished artifact back to underlying logic so that weaknesses can be exposed and stronger versions can be built. The lifecycle follows seven stages: Research and Reconnaissance, Disassembly and Dissection, Analysis and Documentation, Redesign and Conceptualization, Rebuild and Implementation, Testing and Validation, and Redistribution and Release. Each stage is executed with clear mission definition, legal and ethical boundaries, clean-room methods, and measurable validation. The goal is not imitation. The goal is improved function, greater resilience, and open release of the result.

Both the learn-unlearn-relearn cycle and the Reverse Engineering Lifecycle govern this training. Capability under pressure depends on the readiness to acquire what is required, release what no longer works, and install what produces stronger results.
